tldr: Digital self-regulation in Indonesia and Malaysia is culturally embedded in collectivist norms, religious morality, and communal accountability, challenging Western-centric ethical models.
problem_and_motivation: Digital ethics frameworks are predominantly Western and individualistic, failing to account for how cultural values shape self-regulation in non-Western contexts. Existing studies focus on legal regulation or literacy but neglect the cultural-psychological mechanisms influencing everyday online conduct. This gap is critical for understanding ethical behavior in collectivist Southeast Asian societies like Indonesia and Malaysia.
approach:

  - Developed a comparative framework highlighting similarities and differences in self-regulation mechanisms between Indonesia and Malaysia.

  - Used secondary data exclusively, focusing on conceptual depth and theoretical relevance rather than empirical generalization.
findings:
  - num: Indonesia has 143 million active social media users (50.2% of the population), while Malaysia has 25.1 million (70.2%).
  - Digital ethical behavior in both countries is shaped by collectivism, religious morality, and culturally specific mechanisms like shame and social harmony.
  - Self-regulation is socially embedded, driven by community monitoring, reputational concern, and fear of social sanctions rather than legal compliance alone.
  - In Malaysia, self-regulation is more strongly associated with restraint and self-censorship to preserve inter-ethnic harmony, influenced by legal and political boundaries.
  - Indonesia exhibits more expressive public interaction, with self-regulation shaped by community-based monitoring and moral peer pressure.
  - Algorithms and platform architectures often reward provocative content, undermining reflective self-regulation and creating tension with local cultural norms.
  - Role models such as influencers and religious figures significantly shape ethical conduct through observational learning.
  - Digital literacy programs in both countries are primarily reactive and instructional, focusing on rules rather than fostering deep moral reflection.
