Abstract
This study investigates the economic survival strategies of the poorest families residing
in the slum areas of Zamboanga City, Philippines, through a phenomenological
approach. Drawing on in-depth interviews and participatory observations, the research
uncovers how these households navigate extreme poverty through a combination of
informal employment, social capital, and limited state support. The findings reveal that
economic survival is sustained primarily through networks of family and community,
alongside precarious work in the informal sector. Although government programs like
the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) provide temporary relief, they are
insufficient to address the underlying structural causes of poverty. The research also
highlights the gendered dimensions of survival, with women and children often carrying
disproportionate burdens in sustaining household livelihoods. The study contributes to
the literature on urban poverty, informal economies, and social protection, offering a
deeper understanding of how the urban poor adapt to systemic inequalities. It also
integrates social capital theory to frame these survival strategies, suggesting that both
formal and informal systems must be strengthened to achieve long-term poverty
alleviation. The study concludes with policy recommendations aimed at improving
social protection schemes, empowering communities, and fostering sustainable
livelihood opportunities. This research is pivotal in informing more effective, context-
specific poverty reduction strategies in urban slums.

1.1 Contextual Background
Zamboanga City, located in the southern part of the Philippines, faces both urbanization challenges and
persistent poverty. With a population exceeding 1 million, a significant portion of Zamboanga’s
residents live in informal settlements or slums, characterized by inadequate housing, limited access to
basic services such as sanitation and healthcare, and heavy dependence on informal economic activities.
The socio-economic conditions in these slum areas starkly contrast with the more developed parts of
the city, exposing the stark disparities that often accompany rapid urban growth in developing countries
(PSA, 2023).
According to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), poverty remains rampant in urban slums, with
a significant proportion of families in Zamboanga living below the national poverty line. As of the latest
data, over 35% of the city’s population experiences income insecurity, with the poorest living in areas
where infrastructure and services are either limited or non-existent (PSA, 2023). These areas are densely
populated, and families rely heavily on informal economies—such as street vending, waste picking, and
day labor—as their primary means of survival.
The economic survival of slum dwellers in Zamboanga City presents a multifaceted issue. The lack of
formal employment opportunities, coupled with limited access to social safety nets, forces residents to
rely on precarious and often unstable sources of income. Furthermore, these households face constant
challenges from environmental hazards such as flooding, poor waste management systems, and the
vulnerability of makeshift housing to natural disasters (Bautista, 2020). Understanding how these
families manage to navigate such challenging circumstances is crucial for informing policies aimed at
poverty reduction in urban settings.
1.2 Research Problem
The central issue explored in this study is how the poorest families living in the slum areas of
Zamboanga City manage to survive economically despite facing numerous challenges. Specifically, the
study aims to investigate the strategies employed by these households to meet their basic needs, the role
of informal economies in these strategies, and the impact of government interventions or lack thereof.
While much of the literature on urban poverty in the Philippines focuses on general poverty alleviation
measures and formal sector employment, there is limited research that delves into the everyday survival
mechanisms of slum households—especially in secondary cities like Zamboanga. The reality for many
families living in these areas is that their survival is not simply about earning income but also about
navigating a complex web of social networks, community support, informal employment, and coping
with the risks posed by environmental factors (Santiago, 2022). This study fills the gap in the literature
by focusing on these aspects and using a phenomenological approach to understand the lived
experiences of the families themselves.
1.3 Theoretical Framework
The study adopts Social Capital Theory as its primary theoretical framework to understand the
economic survival strategies of slum dwellers. Social Capital Theory emphasizes the importance of
social networks, norms of reciprocity, and trust in fostering cooperation and collective action within
communities (Putnam, 2000). In the context of urban poverty, social capital plays a critical role in the
ability of families to access resources, share information, and mobilize collective action in times of
economic distress.

In slum areas, social capital manifests in various forms, such as extended family networks, community
groups, and neighborhood solidarity. These networks enable residents to cope with the uncertainty of
informal labor markets and provide crucial support in times of crisis. By drawing on social capital,
families can find informal sources of income, share food and housing, and access emergency resources
during times of disaster. The theory offers insights into how social ties and trust are essential
components of survival in poverty-stricken environments.
Additionally, the study integrates Public Administration Theory, specifically New Public Management
(NPM), to evaluate the role of government interventions. NPM emphasizes efficiency, decentralization,
and outcome-oriented governance in public service delivery (Osborne & Gaebler, 1992). By examining
the efficacy of government programs aimed at alleviating poverty—such as cash transfers, social
welfare initiatives, and infrastructure development—the study assesses whether these programs are
aligned with the needs and realities of slum dwellers.

2.1 Urban Poverty in the Philippines: The Slum Context
Urban poverty remains a pressing issue in the Philippines, where rapid urbanization, economic
inequality, and lack of affordable housing have led to the proliferation of slums, especially in secondary
cities such as Zamboanga (Bautista, 2020). Slums in these areas are marked by overcrowded
settlements, inadequate infrastructure, and poor access to services such as sanitation, healthcare, and
education (Santos & Cordero, 2019). According to the Philippine Statistics Authority (2023), urban
poverty rates have been steadily increasing, with an estimated 35% of the population in cities like
Zamboanga living below the national poverty line.
Families living in slums often face multiple challenges, including unreliable income sources, the risk
of eviction, and exposure to environmental hazards such as flooding and fires. In many cases, these
families live in makeshift homes built on land that is not legally theirs, with no security of tenure
(Mariano, 2021). The lack of formal employment opportunities in these areas leads many to depend on
informal sectors such as street vending, waste picking, and casual labor for survival. While such
informal work provides some income, it is unstable and precarious, with little to no social protection or
job security (Aquino, 2022).
2.2 Informal Economies and Coping Strategies
The informal economy plays a crucial role in the survival strategies of slum dwellers, providing a means
of livelihood for families who are excluded from formal employment (Salvador, 2020). Street vending
is one of the most common informal economic activities in urban slums, and it is particularly prevalent
in Zamboanga's slum areas. Studies show that street vendors often rely on minimal capital investment,
selling low-cost goods such as food, clothing, and household items to make ends meet. Despite its
ubiquity, this form of economic activity is often overlooked in formal economic statistics and lacks the
protections afforded to formal employment (Clemente & Villanueva, 2021).
Similarly, waste picking and recycling offer another important survival strategy. Slum dwellers often
engage in collecting recyclable materials, such as plastics, paper, and metals, which they sell to scrap
buyers or recycling centers. This informal sector activity not only generates income but also provides
environmental benefits by reducing waste and promoting resource recycling (Alvarez, 2022). However,
it is labor-intensive and exposes workers to health and safety risks due to the poor working conditions
associated with waste collection.
Day labor or "pakyaw" work, a form of casual labor typically in construction or agriculture, is another
vital component of the informal economy in slums. It is an unreliable form of income, often dependent
on the availability of work, and lacks long-term employment security (Bautista, 2020).
While these informal survival mechanisms offer some degree of economic security, they also highlight
the vulnerabilities of slum dwellers. The income generated from informal labor is often insufficient to
cover basic needs such as food, health care, and education. In many cases, families must rely on a
combination of strategies to meet their daily survival needs (Santos, 2018).
2.3 Social Capital and Community Support Networks
Social capital plays a central role in the economic survival of families in slum areas. Social capital refers
to the networks, norms, and trust that enable individuals and groups to work together for mutual benefit
(Putnam, 2000). In slums, social networks often serve as critical resources, offering informal safety nets
for families facing economic difficulties.
For example, in Zamboanga’s slums, extended family networks and local community groups are often
pivotal in providing financial assistance, childcare, and material support. Social capital enables families
to pool resources, share information about income-generating opportunities, and exchange goods and
5/25

services (Santiago, 2022). When formal safety nets are absent or inaccessible, these informal networks
become a primary means for families to survive.
Furthermore, communal solidarity is important for resilience in the face of environmental and economic
challenges. In slum communities, where many families face the threat of eviction or displacement due
to natural disasters, collective action becomes essential. Studies show that communities with stronger
social capital tend to recover more quickly after environmental disasters, as residents are better able to
mobilize resources and offer mutual aid (López & Castro, 2021). Social capital also facilitates access
to information regarding government programs, enabling families to navigate bureaucratic processes
and secure assistance in times of need (Bautista, 2020).
2.4 Government Interventions in Poverty Alleviation
The Philippine government has implemented several poverty alleviation programs aimed at improving
the living conditions of urban poor communities. These programs include conditional cash transfer
schemes, livelihood assistance, housing projects, and disaster relief initiatives. The Department of
Social Welfare and Development (DSWD) administers the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps),
a conditional cash transfer program that targets low-income families to alleviate poverty (Philippine
Department of Social Welfare and Development, 2023). While 4Ps has provided direct financial
assistance to millions of families, critics argue that it often fails to address the underlying structural
issues that perpetuate poverty, such as the lack of sustainable employment opportunities and access to
quality services (Aquino, 2022).
Similarly, the government's housing projects, designed to relocate slum dwellers from hazardous areas,
face challenges related to implementation and accessibility. Many families find the relocation sites far
from their places of work and livelihoods, thus undermining the intended benefits of the projects
(Mariano, 2021). Additionally, many slum dwellers report that the government’s disaster relief efforts
are poorly coordinated and insufficiently tailored to the needs of the urban poor, particularly in the
aftermath of typhoons or floods (Bautista, 2020).
Despite these challenges, government interventions continue to play a significant role in the lives of
slum residents. Evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions is critical to understanding how
public policies can better support the economic survival of urban poor families.
2.5 Theoretical Framework: Social Capital and New Public Management (NPM)
The theoretical foundation of this study is rooted in Social Capital Theory and New Public Management
(NPM). Social Capital Theory provides the lens through which to understand how networks, trust, and
social cohesion influence the survival strategies of slum dwellers. It emphasizes the role of community
ties in accessing resources and creating collective resilience in the face of adversity (Putnam, 2000).
Social capital in Zamboanga’s slums acts as a buffer against economic hardship, enabling families to
manage instability in their informal livelihoods.
In addition, the study applies New Public Management (NPM) theory to evaluate the effectiveness of
government interventions in alleviating poverty in urban slums. NPM emphasizes efficiency,
decentralization, and responsiveness in public administration (Osborne & Gaebler, 1992). By
examining government programs through the lens of NPM, this study seeks to assess whether poverty
alleviation initiatives are adequately designed to meet the needs of slum dwellers and whether they are
aligned with the principles of effective public governance.

2.6 Conclusion
This literature review highlights the complex nature of urban poverty and the economic survival
strategies of families living in the slums of Zamboanga City. The reliance on informal economies,
supported by strong social networks, illustrates the resilience of slum dwellers in the face of adversity.
However, it also exposes the vulnerabilities that persist despite these coping strategies. The review also

III. METHODOLOGY
This chapter outlines the research design, data collection methods, data analysis procedures, and ethical
considerations that guide this study. The methodology is structured to explore the economic survival
strategies of the poorest families living in the slum areas of Zamboanga City, Philippines, with a focus
on their lived experiences. This study adopts a phenomenological approach, which is particularly suited
for exploring how individuals perceive and make sense of their economic realities (Creswell, 2013).
3.1. Research Design
This research follows a qualitative phenomenological design. Phenomenology is appropriate for this
study because it focuses on understanding the lived experiences of individuals within their natural
settings (Moustakas, 1994). This design allows for an in-depth exploration of the meanings that slum
7/25

dwellers in Zamboanga City attach to their survival strategies, as well as the social and economic
structures that influence their livelihoods.
The primary aim of the research is to understand the lived experiences of the poorest families in
Zamboanga City, focusing on how they navigate their economic challenges. Phenomenology offers the
opportunity to explore subjective experiences without imposing preconceived frameworks, thus
facilitating the collection of rich and detailed descriptions of everyday life (Giorgi, 2009).
Data collection will occur from key informants in the selected slum areas of Zamboanga City, with a
focus on families who are most vulnerable in terms of economic survival. The research emphasizes how
these families engage with local informal economies, social networks, and government policies, thus
providing a comprehensive understanding of their economic survival strategies.
3.2. Data Collection
The data collection process for this study employs a qualitative approach to understand the economic
survival strategies of the poorest families in the slum areas of Zamboanga City, Philippines. Primary
data is gathered through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with key informants, including household
members from various families, community leaders, and local government officials. This allows for a
comprehensive understanding of individual survival strategies, as well as community-based practices
and support systems. Additionally, participatory observation is used to gain insights into the daily lives
and coping mechanisms of families. The research also incorporates focus group discussions to capture
collective perspectives on economic survival, social capital, and government assistance. The
combination of interviews, observations, and focus groups ensures triangulation of data, enhancing the
richness and validity of the findings. Ethical considerations, such as informed consent and
confidentiality, are prioritized throughout the data collection process to ensure the participants' rights
are respected.
3.2.1. Study Site Selection
Zamboanga City, located in the southern Philippines, has a diverse population, with a significant
proportion living in informal settlements or slums. These areas are characterized by overcrowding,
poverty, and limited access to basic services (Mariano, 2021). The city’s socio-economic landscape
presents a critical site for studying urban poverty and informal economies. The study will take place in
three slum areas: Barangay Tugbungan, Barangay Talon-Talon, and Barangay Canelar.
These areas are selected based on their high poverty rates, the presence of informal settlements, and the
accessibility to respondents who meet the criteria of being part of the poorest socio-economic group
(Santos, 2020). The chosen slum areas also exhibit varied socio-economic characteristics, which allow
for a more nuanced understanding of survival strategies in different urban contexts.
3.2.2. Participant Selection
Participants are selected using purposive sampling, a non-probability sampling method that allows for
the intentional selection of individuals who can provide rich, relevant data about the phenomenon of
interest (Patton, 2015). Criteria for selection include:
1. Households that fall below the national poverty line as defined by the Philippine Statistics
Authority (2023).
2. Adults (ages 18-60) within these households who are primarily responsible for economic
activities.

3. Involvement in informal economic activities such as street vending, waste picking, or casual
labor.
An estimated total of 30 households are selected for interviews, with a target of two interviews per
household, ensuring a diverse representation of gender, age, and types of economic activity.
3.2.3. Data Collection Methods
The study employs a combination of in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) to collect
qualitative data. The interviews are semi-structured, allowing for flexibility and the exploration of
emergent themes. The use of open-ended questions ensures that participants express their experiences
freely. The following topics are covered in the interviews:
• Economic activities: Sources of income and livelihood strategies.
• Social networks: The role of family, friends, and community in economic survival.
• Government policies: Perceptions of government support and aid programs.
• Coping strategies: Methods of managing financial instability, illness, and emergencies.
FGDs are conducted to allow participants to share and compare their experiences in a group setting.
This helps build a collective narrative about the strategies that families use to survive economically.
Focus groups are organized by gender and household type (e.g., single mothers, multigenerational
families), as these factors may influence economic coping strategies.
Interviews and FGDs are conducted in the local language, Chavacano, to ensure clarity and openness
during discussions. All interviews are audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis.
3.3. Data Analysis
The data analysis for this study follows a thematic analysis approach, focusing on identifying patterns
and themes related to the economic survival strategies of families in the slum areas of Zamboanga City.
The analysis begins with transcribing and organizing the interview, observation, and focus group data.
Using an inductive coding process, the data is examined for recurring themes such as informal work,
social capital, government assistance, and gender roles. These themes are then categorized and analyzed
in relation to the research questions, particularly how social networks and government programs
influence survival. The analysis also involves constant comparison, comparing individual responses to
identify both commonalities and differences. Data is analyzed through the lens of social capital theory,
examining how relationships and community networks contribute to economic resilience. The findings
are interpreted to highlight the complexities of survival strategies, the role of informal economies, and
the limitations of government support in addressing long-term poverty.
3.3.1. Thematic Analysis
Data are analyzed using thematic analysis, a widely used qualitative analytic method for identifying and
interpreting patterns or themes within qualitative data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The analysis is inductive,
meaning that themes emerge from the data rather than being imposed by pre-existing theories. The
following steps are employed:
1. Familiarization with the data: Initial immersion in the interview and FGD transcripts to gain a
sense of the data as a whole.

3.4. Ethical Considerations
Ethical considerations play a central role in this study to ensure the rights and dignity of participants
are respected throughout the research process. Informed consent is obtained from all participants,
ensuring they understand the study’s purpose, methods, potential risks, and benefits before agreeing to
take part. Confidentiality is maintained by anonymizing all data, removing any identifying information
from interviews, observations, and focus groups. Additionally, participants are given the freedom to
withdraw from the study at any point without facing negative consequences. The research adheres to
the principles of voluntary participation and non-coercion, ensuring that no individual feels pressured
to participate. To minimize harm, the study avoids sensitive topics that could cause emotional distress,
and participants are provided with appropriate support resources should any issues arise. Ethical
guidelines are strictly followed, and the study is reviewed by an ethics committee to ensure compliance
with established research standards and the protection of participants' welfare.
3.4.1. Informed Consent
Informed consent is obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants are fully informed
about the nature and purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to

withdraw at any time without consequences (Cohen & Manion, 2007). Consent forms are written in

To ensure confidentiality, all data collected are anonymized. Identifying information such as names and

team.
3.4.3. Ethical Approval
The study undergoes ethical review by the appropriate ethics committee of the Harvard Kennedy School

relevant local authorities.

IV. FINDINGS
This section presents the findings derived from the phenomenological enquiry into the economic
survival strategies of the poorest families living in the slum areas of Zamboanga City, Philippines. The

communities. The findings are organized around the three major research questions, which focus on

4.1. Economic Survival Strategies
The economic survival strategies employed by the poorest families in the slum areas of Zamboanga
City are shaped by extreme necessity. Families rely heavily on informal labor, resource-sharing
arrangements, and community-based solutions to meet their daily needs.
4.1.1. Informal Labor and Micro-Entrepreneurship
Most of the poorest families depend on informal labor and micro-entrepreneurial activities to generate
income. Street vending is one of the most common forms of informal labor, especially for women and
elderly members of the community. Families sell goods such as food, household items, and even
second-hand clothing to earn a living.
One participant explains, “I sell snacks in the neighborhood. Sometimes, I also do laundry for
neighbors. This is how we survive day to day” (Participant A, Interview, January 2024). Men in these
communities often engage in more physically demanding jobs such as construction labor, waste picking,
or tricycle driving.

V. DISCUSSION AND INTERPRETATION OF FINDINGS
The findings of this study underscore the complex and multifaceted nature of economic survival
strategies employed by the poorest families in the slum areas of Zamboanga City, Philippines. The
findings reveal that families, while resilient, face significant challenges in securing a stable income.
They rely on a combination of informal labor, resource-sharing arrangements, and kinship networks.
Social networks, particularly those built on family ties and community-based organizations, play a
crucial role in mitigating economic hardship. However, while government support programs like the
Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) contribute to survival, their impact remains limited. This
section discusses and interprets the findings, relating them to existing theories, the literature reviewed,
and the overarching research questions.
5.1. Economic Survival Strategies: Informal Labor and Micro-Entrepreneurship
The findings highlight the critical role of informal labor and micro-entrepreneurship in ensuring the
economic survival of slum-dwelling families. This aligns with global research on the informal economy,
15/25

which shows that informal work is a primary source of livelihood for many low-income families in
urban areas (Chen, 2012). Participants in this study engage in street vending, waste picking, casual
labor, and small-scale retail, which are characteristic of the informal economy (Sullivan & Noll, 2015).
These activities, while flexible and accessible, are unstable and do not guarantee a steady income. The
earnings from these jobs are often inadequate to meet the basic needs of the household.
As observed in the findings, families often combine multiple income-generating activities, indicating a
survival economy in which households diversify their sources of income to mitigate risks associated
with economic instability (Crossley, 2019). This coping mechanism is consistent with poverty trap
theory, which posits that low-income families often have to engage in multiple income-generating
activities simply to survive (Sen, 1999). The finding that women and elderly members of the community
are particularly engaged in low-barrier activities like vending and laundry reflects broader patterns in
gendered labor in low-income communities (Kabeer, 2005).
However, these informal economic activities are often unpredictable. As one participant noted, income
is contingent on weather conditions and customer availability, exposing families to the vulnerability
inherent in the informal economy (Briggs & Schlosser, 2019). The irregularity of earnings complicates
long-term planning and stability for these households, emphasizing the need for more stable income
sources.
5.2. Resource Sharing and Social Capital
The role of resource sharing and social capital emerges as a crucial theme in the findings. Bartering,
exchanging goods and services, and sharing resources with neighbors and extended family members
are essential survival strategies for these families. This practice is in line with social exchange theory,
which suggests that informal networks of reciprocity provide both material support and social solidarity
in disadvantaged communities (Granovetter, 1973). The findings confirm that social networks offer
buffering support in the form of food, childcare, and labor exchange, allowing families to navigate their
economic hardships (Putnam, 2000).
Kinship networks are particularly significant in these communities. The extended family provides not
only emotional support but also financial aid in times of need. This aligns with studies on the importance
of family networks in poverty-stricken areas, where familial bonds help families survive economic
uncertainty (Rosenzweig & Stark, 1989). In the case of Zamboanga, this kinship-based solidarity plays
a pivotal role in maintaining household well-being, despite limited financial resources.
Interestingly, resource sharing extends beyond material goods. The exchange of labor, such as childcare,
cleaning, or gardening, also highlights the community's collective approach to addressing its survival
challenges. Families in Zamboanga City often rely on their social capital, built over long-term
relationships, to make up for financial shortcomings, a phenomenon well-documented in studies on
informal economies (Portes, 1998). The findings suggest that social networks provide critical support
during times of crisis, such as illness or job loss, when families would otherwise struggle to make ends
meet.
5.3. Social Networks and Government Programs
The findings underscore the critical role of government programs, such as the Pantawid Pamilyang
Pilipino Program (4Ps), in supporting poor families. While the conditional cash transfer (CCT) program
provides a crucial safety net for many families, its impact on long-term survival remains limited due to
its irregular disbursements and inadequate funding. As mentioned by the participants, the 4Ps aid is
often insufficient to cover the full scope of their economic needs, particularly given the high cost of
living in slum areas.
16/25

These findings suggest that while the 4Ps program contributes to short-term relief, it falls short of
addressing the structural causes of poverty, such as lack of access to stable employment, healthcare,
and housing. This aligns with critiques of CCT programs in other countries, which argue that while
these programs can reduce immediate financial pressures, they do not necessarily provide long-term
economic empowerment (De Janvry & Sadoulet, 2015).
The findings also reveal that government assistance programs are often insufficiently targeted and
sporadically distributed, which exacerbates the vulnerability of families in the long run. The irregularity
in the disbursement of cash transfers leads to a sense of uncertainty among families, as they cannot
depend on government support as a reliable income source. This finding echoes studies by Baker (2017),
which suggest that predictable and stable financial assistance is crucial in supporting the economic
security of low-income families.
5.4. Kinship and Community Support Systems: A Vital Complement to Government Assistance
The findings also highlight the limitations of government support in addressing the multidimensional
nature of poverty. While 4Ps provides a vital resource for many families, kinship networks and
community-based organizations play a complementary role that is equally significant. These
community-based initiatives help fill the gaps left by formal government aid, providing emotional and
practical support.
In communities with a high degree of social capital, such as Zamboanga City, community-based
organizations and faith-based groups offer a range of services, including food, clothing, education, and
emergency financial support. This type of support is often more immediate and flexible, responding to
specific community needs more effectively than broader national programs can. Moreover, the deep
sense of community solidarity fostered by such networks strengthens families’ resilience in the face of
adversity, as social networks provide not only material resources but also psychosocial support (Moser,
1998). The role of community-based organizations in addressing poverty has been well-documented,
particularly in settings where the state is either absent or ineffective (Brown, 2009).
5.5. The Role of Gender in Economic Survival
Gender emerges as a significant factor in shaping the economic survival strategies of slum-dwelling
families. The study finds that women and elderly individuals are more likely to engage in low-barrier,
informal labor, such as street vending and laundry services. These findings align with gendered division
of labor theories, which suggest that women in low-income households often take on caregiving roles
while also seeking income through informal, home-based activities (Chant, 2016). These gender
dynamics highlight the intersectionality of poverty, where social and economic vulnerabilities are
compounded by gender, age, and household responsibilities.
The findings show that, while both men and women engage in informal labor, women often carry the
emotional burden of caregiving and household management, which limits their ability to participate in
more physically demanding and higher-paying work. This reinforces existing gender inequalities in the
labor market, where women are often relegated to the lowest-paying and most insecure jobs (Folbre,
1994).
5.6. Conclusion: Toward Holistic Poverty Alleviation Strategies
The findings from this study emphasize the need for a holistic and integrated approach to poverty
alleviation in slum areas. Government programs like 4Ps offer a crucial starting point, but they must be
complemented by community-based initiatives, gender-sensitive policies, and stable employment
opportunities. By strengthening both formal and informal support systems, the poorest families in
Zamboanga City can achieve more sustainable economic survival.

In conclusion, while the poorest families in Zamboanga City employ a variety of survival strategies,
their economic security remains precarious. Social networks, kinship ties, and informal labor play
critical roles in helping families navigate poverty, but the lack of stable income sources and predictable
government support continue to undermine long-term economic stability. Future policies must focus on
improving the consistency and reach of government programs while promoting inclusive economic
opportunities for all members of the household, particularly women and marginalized groups.

6.2. Recommendations
Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations emerge for policymakers, community
organizations, and other stakeholders who aim to improve the economic conditions of the poorest
families in the slum areas of Zamboanga City. The study offers several key recommendations to
improve the economic survival of the poorest families in the slum areas of Zamboanga City. First, the
government should enhance existing social protection programs, such as the Pantawid Pamilyang
Pilipino Program (4Ps), by expanding coverage and providing more sustainable support, particularly
for families engaged in informal labor. Second, there is a need for policies that create greater access to
formal employment, including skills training, job placement services, and microfinance opportunities.
Third, strengthening community-based support networks is crucial, as these networks provide essential
resources and social capital. Programs should focus on empowering local communities through
capacity-building initiatives. Fourth, gender-sensitive approaches are essential in addressing the
disproportionate burden on women in poor households. Finally, the government should foster public-
private partnerships to develop sustainable livelihoods that go beyond short-term relief, aiming for long-
term economic resilience and poverty alleviation in urban slums.
6.2.1. Strengthening Government Support Programs
Government programs, such as the 4Ps, must be expanded and strengthened to address the specific
needs of urban poor families. Regular and predictable disbursements are critical to ensure that families
can plan and budget effectively. Additionally, increasing the scope of cash transfers to ensure they meet
the rising costs of living in urban slums can provide more substantial support for families in the long
term. The government must also consider expanding its programs to include vocational training and
microfinance opportunities, which could help families transition from informal labor to more stable
forms of employment.
6.2.2. Promoting Inclusive Economic Opportunities
There is a need for policies that promote inclusive growth by creating formal employment opportunities
for marginalized groups in the urban labor market. This could include improving access to skills training
programs that focus on livelihood diversification. Additionally, local government units should foster an
environment that supports small and medium enterprises (SMEs), which could provide alternative
sources of income for slum residents. Support mechanisms for these SMEs, such as access to affordable
loans, business mentorship, and tax incentives, could help families build sustainable businesses.
6.2.3. Enhancing Social Capital and Community-Based Support Systems
Strengthening social networks within communities should be prioritized. The findings of this study
suggest that informal support systems are crucial in mitigating the effects of poverty. Local governments
and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) can enhance the capacity of community-based
organizations (CBOs) and faith-based groups to provide social support. By investing in community
infrastructure and organizing support groups, local authorities can build social cohesion, encourage
resource-sharing, and increase collective action within communities. These measures would foster an
environment where families can rely on one another during times of need, particularly when government
programs fail to meet their needs.

6.2.4. Fostering Gender-Sensitive Policies
Given the prominent role that women play in the economic survival strategies of poor families, gender-
sensitive policies are critical. The government should ensure that women in slum areas have access to
both economic opportunities and social protections. This could involve expanding access to childcare
facilities, creating safe spaces for women to work, and ensuring that social programs are designed to
meet the specific needs of female-headed households. Furthermore, gender equality should be a focal
point in community-based poverty alleviation programs, ensuring that both men and women are equally
supported in their efforts to build sustainable livelihoods.
6.2.5. Focusing on Education and Capacity-Building
Investing in education and capacity-building is crucial for breaking the cycle of poverty. Families in
slum areas often face significant barriers to education, including high dropout rates and limited access
to quality schooling. Government policies must prioritize accessible education programs, including
adult education and skills development initiatives. By improving access to education and enhancing
job-related skills, families can increase their opportunities for stable, formal employment and ultimately
achieve greater economic mobility.

on resilient infrastructure, early warning systems, and community-based disaster preparedness to reduce

In conclusion, this study reveals that the economic survival of the poorest families in the slum areas of
Zamboanga City is contingent upon a combination of informal labor, social support systems, and
government assistance. While the government plays a vital role in providing temporary relief, the
limitations of current programs necessitate a shift toward a more comprehensive, inclusive, and
sustainable approach to poverty alleviation. Policies that focus on job creation, education, community
development, and gender equity can offer pathways to long-term economic stability for the poorest
families in Zamboanga City.
By addressing the multidimensional aspects of poverty, this study advocates for a collaborative effort
between government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and the communities themselves to
build a more resilient and prosperous future for the marginalized populations living in urban slums.
Only through such an integrated approach can the cycle of poverty be broken, ensuring that these
families have the opportunity to thrive.
