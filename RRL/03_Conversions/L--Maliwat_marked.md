ABSTRACT
In this paper, I investigate how inequalities in permanent income affects the saving
behaviors of Philippine households. I exploit the instrumental variable method to
formalize an empirical model that enables me to determine how permanent and
transitory income affects the household saving rate across subsamples of households. I
find that, unlike upper-class households, lower- and middle-class households are likely
to spend large enough portions of additional permanent income to reduce their saving
rates. Meanwhile, all households regardless of income classification tend to save larger
portions of additional transitory income. This suggests that households are driven to
spend and to save contingent on how they perceive the permanency or transitoriness
of additional income to be. I also find preliminary evidence that, on average,
households do attempt to spend prudently as they spend more of their permanent
incomes on essential and recurrent expenditure items such as those on food, housing,
transportation, and communication. My results show the overarching contribution of
permanent income in determining household saving and importance of policy
interventions to improve the low-savings economic environment of the Philippines by
at least revolving around raising permanent income levels or alleviating consumption-
side pressures of households.

1 Introduction
Philippine authorities and researchers have long been cognizant of low saving patterns
among Philippine households. Under commission by the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas
(BSP), Bersales & Mapa (2006) find that, from 1985 to 2003, the country recorded its
lowest (absolute) household saving rate of 16 percent in 2003. Notably, they find that
the trend of declining household saving rates persisted among households across all
levels of income save for those in the highest income group. Terada-Hagiwara (2009)
reports the same trend, noting that the country posted a national saving rate (as a
share of gross domestic product) of 2 percent in 2007, a far cry from the 10 percent
recorded in 2002.
Though marginal improvements have been seen since then, the household savings
situation in the country remains to be an area of policy concern. The BSP identifies the
low-savings environment to be one of the primary forces that hinders financial inclusion
in the country. In its report on the results of the 2019 Financial Inclusion Survey, the BSP
(2021) finds that almost half (47 percent) of Philippine adults did not have savings in
the reference period. The lack of money (or income) was cited as the foremost reason
for the inability to save.
Uncoincidentally, the incidence of saving is higher among those who are employed,
those who have family members working abroad, and those who own their own
businesses—those who have reliable sources of income. Saving incidence also
increases along higher socioeconomic classes; around 79 percent of upper- to upper-
middle-class households have savings, while only 48 percent (below half) of the
poorest households have savings.
Clearly, the importance of income when it comes to household saving behaviors
cannot be overstated. Investigating income-savings interplays among Philippine
households thus is warranted.
In the literature of household income and saving, one oft-cited theory stands out in
pertinence to this subject matter: the permanent income hypothesis (Friedman, 1957).
The hypothesis posits that household income is made of two components: the
permanent component, which is the expected average value of lifetime income
streams; and the permanent component, which refers to unanticipated windfall income
shocks. A key proposition of the hypothesis is that household consumption depends
on permanent income alone. Thus, if households receive positive windfall incomes,
they increase their savings. Conversely, if they experience downward income shocks,
they dissave as they anticipate their incomes to return to their permanent path in the
future.
In this study, I explore the consequences of the permanent income hypothesis on the
saving behaviors of Philippine households under the context of stark income
inequalities that plague the nation. I formulate an empirical model that uses the
instrumental variable method to estimate permanent and transitory income
components and to assess how these components determine the household saving
rate across subsamples of households grouped according to income class.

Consistent with other studies in the literature, I find that investigating the income-
savings relationship using observed measures of household income tends to
overestimate how income affects saving, which potentially leads researchers to make
erroneous conclusions about how households save when faced with additional income.
The marginal effect of observed income on the household saving rate is dominated by
the effect of the transitory component of income, rather than the permanent. If
household savings were driven largely by transitory income, it is careless to conclude
that households have the capacity to save regularly. This is particularly important when
income inequality is considered as I find that lower- and middle-class households tend
to spend large portions of additional permanent income to reduce their saving rates
and less of transitory income shocks to increase the same measure.
I also find that additional permanent and transitory income both result in increased
levels of spending. This is especially apparent for lower- and middle-class households.
Notably, I find that the marginal propensities to consume out of permanent income
among lower- and middle-class households are large enough to reduce their saving
rates. In fact, the marginal propensity to consume out of permanent income is greater
than unity for lower-class households. Meanwhile, the marginal propensities to
consume out of transitory income are small enough such that households are able to
increase their saving rates. Taken together, these results indicate that households are
driven to spend and, conversely, to save based on precautionary motives; their
perceptions about the permanency or transitoriness of income influence spending
confidence.
I also find initial evidence that points to the existence of some degree of prudence in
spending among households across all income classes. Lower- and middle-class
households seem to use additional levels of permanent income for essential and
regular expenditure items and transitory shocks for likewise necessary but less
frequently incurred items.
My results show the overarching significance of permanent income when it comes to
household saving. Data from official government surveys have all but confirmed this
sentiment. Thus, policy interventions to improve the poor household savings situation
in the Philippines should be geared towards increasing permanent income levels or
relieving consumption pressures of households to effectively increase disposable
permanent income. On the fiscal side, this entails preventing cost-push inflationary
shocks, implementing developmental interventions to increase permanent income
levels of households at the aggregate, and carefully setting consumption tax rates to
prevent disproportionately affecting the poor. On the central bank’s side, this means
fulfilling the mandate of price stability and continuing financial education campaigns
but with permanent income inequality considerations in mind.

2 Review of related literature
The centrality of income as a determinant of household savings is extensively
investigated in contemporary literature on household savings. A popular strand of the
literature deals with the question on how income inequality determines differences in
household saving behaviors through the lens of the permanent income hypothesis
(Friedman, 1957).
Authors investigating the income-saving relationship with the permanent income
hypothesis in mind argue against using current (observed) income as a determinant of
the household saving rate. They concur with Friedman (1957) in that there is the need
to, in the words of Dynan et al. (2004), “distinguish those [households] with high
lifetime income from those whose income is high only transitorily” and “to correct for
measurement error in income”, because positive transitory income shocks and upward
measurement errors will tend to overstate the effect of measured income on
household savings.1
Bebczuk et al. (2015) and Dynan et al. (2004), for example, demonstrate the presence
of upward bias from transitory income and/or measurement errors by regressing
household savings against current and permanent income individually in separate
model specifications. They find that the effect of current income on household saving is
generally greater than that of permanent income for countries in Latin America and the
United States, respectively. This treatment of this particular matter is not new, of
course, as even authors of much older studies such as Bautista & Lamberte (1990) and
Rodriguez & Meyer (1988) perform tests of the same kind and find similar conclusions
with regard to the presence of bias in marginal effects by using current measures of
income instead of estimates of permanent income.
Isolating the effect of permanent income on household saving (versus that of current) is
more significant when income inequality is considered. This is made evident in the
studies of Zabot & Gomes (2021) and Gandelman (2017), whose results make it
apparent that the upward bias of transitory income shocks and/or measurement errors
is manifest mostly for higher-income households in Brazil and in the greater Latin
American region, respectively. Their results also suggest the converse inference that
the marginal effect of income on household saving is biased downward for lower-class
households in the aforesaid regions when current income, rather than permanent, is
used as the regressor.

Moreover, authors that traverse this route of analysis are interested in testing the core
propositions put forth by Friedman (1957). In general, authors investigating this line of
inquiry find themselves at odds with the proportionality hypothesis of the permanent
income hypothesis, which proposes that the proportion of consumption with respect to
permanent income is constant regardless of the level of income. For example, Dynan et
al. (2004) in their influential work conclude decisively that “the rich do, indeed save
more” (based on tests using both current and, more importantly, permanent income as
determinants of household saving). More recent studies likewise find that households
with greater wealth or higher expected lifetime incomes tend to save more than those
without (Zabot & Gomes, 2021; Alvarez-Cuadrado & El-Attar Vilalta, 2018; Bozio et al.,
2017; Gandelman, 2017). Reasons cited include bequest motives, precautionary
saving, and even wealth incurrence merely for the sake of it (Carroll, 2000). Alan et al.
(2015), however, dissent from the conclusions of the aforesaid group of authors as they
find that richer Canadian households do not save more relative to those in the middle
of the permanent income distribution. What they do find, though, is that the poor “in a
lifetime sense” dissave. All things considered, the motivation to analyze the relationship
between permanent income and household saving is established.
There are also authors of older studies who, intentionally or otherwise, challenge the
strict assumption of the permanent income hypothesis that transitory income is wholly
saved. Worth mentioning are the studies of Bautista & Lamberte (1990), Rodriguez &
Meyer (1988), and Abdel-Ghany et al. (1983), who expressly investigate the effect of
transitory income, in addition to permanent, on household saving and find that the
marginal propensity to consume out of transitory income is not nil. The aforesaid
authors also allude to income class considerations, albeit inexplicitly, as they study
heterogeneities in the effects of permanent and transitory income components on the
household saving rate based on differences in certain household characteristics that
indicate in no small part the average income levels of households.
Rodriguez & Meyer (1988), for instance, find that the marginal propensity to save out of
transitory income in fact is greater than that out of permanent income for rural
Philippine households. Bautista & Lamberte (1990) report similar results and find
evidence of heterogeneity in marginal propensities to save (both permanent and
transitory income) contingent on differences in the regional location and on differences
in urbanity of Philippine households. Meanwhile, Abdel-Ghany et al. (1983) likewise
find that the marginal propensity to consume out of transitory income is greater than
the marginal propensity to consume out of permanent income when transitory income
is small relative to permanent income, opening a course for research that has to do
with how the permanent-transitory makeup of income affects household saving.
That the Philippines is a country characterized by stark and persistent income
inequalities and a low-savings macroeconomic environment makes this a crucially
pertinent subject worth investigating. It is thus only fitting that I join other studies within
this important realm of inquiry.

3 Empirical strategy
My study investigates two levels of inquiry regarding the saving behaviors of Philippine
households. At the surface, I conduct a general analysis with the propositions of the
permanent income hypothesis in mind. I then look deeper into the matter by
incorporating income class considerations into the analysis.
In assessing how permanent income affects household consumption and saving
behaviors, the most immediate complication has to do with how permanent income is
estimated in the first place. The instrumental variable method is used extensively in this
strand of the literature to address the problems associated with measurement errors
and transitory shocks contaminating observed income. This approach enables me to
estimate the permanent incomes of households by isolating the variation in observed
income associated with household characteristics that determine the lifetime income
potentials and subsequently evaluate the marginal effect of permanent income on the
household saving rate using these estimates.
I note that the instrumental variable method only enables me to estimate the effect of
permanent income on the household saving rate; by design, it ignores the effect of
transitory income on the same measure. I thus exploit the method only for the
intermediate steps in my analysis, and I rely on conceptual definitions proposed by the
permanent income hypothesis to formalize a more complete empirical model that
estimates how transitory income determines the household saving rate.

4 Data particulars
For my empirical analysis to be consistent with the conceptual definitions provided by
the permanent income hypothesis, the data I use as inputs to my empirical model
should conform with my analytical assumptions. I thus dedicate this section to discuss
my data needs.
My data comes from the 20155 iteration of the Family Income and Expenditure Survey
(FIES) of the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA). The FIES series consists of surveys
geared towards measuring family income and expenditures for policymaking purposes
of the national government (PSA, 2020).
My interest in this particular data set lies in the richness of data on income, its
components, and sources; and on expenditures, their components, and categories;
household characteristics; and demographic information; among others. The 2015
FIES also covers a sample of 41,544 households, providing me a sufficiently
representative account of the Philippine population.
The FIES provides data on household income according to type and source. I do away
with imposing arbitrary notions of permanency or transitoriness of income based on
types and sources and consider the sum total of all incomes as my primary measure of
observed income. In doing so, I allow my estimation strategy to estimate permanent
income from this aggregate measure without prejudice to the aforesaid factors.

In measuring household consumption, I account for expenditures in cash only. The
FIES data set provides for expenditures in kind and as gifts, but analysis finds no need
for these kinds of expenditures. I consider cash expenses to be the only kind that is
taken from household income to determine the level of household saving. For
example, a household whose consumption is entirely in kind or gift-based will have a
saving rate of 100 percent being that none of its income is ever used for expenses.
The income and expenditure data used are scaled downward by a factor of 10,000 for
analytical brevity. 6 As such, a unit increase in income or expenditures refers to an
increase of ₱10,000 for either measure.
As many authors have done before me, I use the college educational attainment of the
household head as my primary instrumental variable. The literature has accepted the
household head’s education to be strongly correlated with lifetime income. I assert that
this holds true especially so in the Philippines. Olfindo (2018) and Acosta et al. (2017),
for example, find that having a college degree is strongly correlated with higher wages
and formal employment in the Philippines. Olfindo (2018), in particular, finds evidence
that Philippine wage workers with a tertiary diploma are paid disproportionately higher
than those without; additionally, the effect of possessing a tertiary diploma does not
diminish with work experience.
Of course, I recognize that the robustness of my results hinges on the reliability of my
permanent income estimates, which, in turn, are sensitive to the choice of instrument I
use (Alan et al., 2015). I therefore conduct additional tests using various other
instruments suggested in the literature to see if my general findings hold even as I alter
the key inputs to the empirical model.
Dynan et al. (2004) offer three other instruments in addition to the education of the
household head: household consumption, lagged labor income, and future labor
income. Zabot & Gomes (2021) and Alan et al. (2015) also consider total consumption
(Alan et al., 2015) and nondurable consumption (Zabot & Gomes, 2021; Alan et al.,
2015). Because my data set does not contain historical nor future expectations data on
income, I am limited to exploring categories of consumption as alternative instruments.
Bebczuk et al. (2015) use the ownership of real assets, particularly the ownership of
homes and automotive vehicles as additional instruments. I follow their reasoning as
intuition tells me that the ability to make big-ticket purchases are likely to be
dependent on the level of permanent income flows. Households will generally rely on
increases in expected lifetime income rather than wait for transitory income shocks to
invest in such real assets that will be utilized for long periods of time. Of course, I do
not discount that considerable windfall income shocks also enable households to

I suppose two households incur the same level of increase in their incomes. I suppose
further that they have the same level of consumption greater than nil. If both
households also have the same level of total income, then the change in their
respective household saving rates will be equal. At this point, the compositional
makeup of income for each household does not matter, and which component of
income increases for which household is inconsequential. However, if their initial
incomes are unequal to begin with, the change in their respective household saving
rates from the same increase in income will likewise be unequal; the household with a
smaller income base will enjoy a larger change in its household saving rate.

For lower- and middle-class households, much of additional permanent and transitory
income go to food and housing. Lower-class households also tend to spend a sizable
portion of additional income on communication expenses (e.g., internet subscriptions,
or prepaid or postpaid mobile cellular plans). In addition, both sets of households

propensity to consume from permanent income is greater than that from transitory
income. It bears noting that these consumption categories are arguably necessary for
survival, routinary in nature, and are thus largely unavoidable, explaining the gap in the
marginal propensities to consume out of permanent and transitory income.
There are four consumption categories in which additional transitory income results in
greater spending than does additional permanent income: education, clothing,
durables, and health. Expenditures of these kinds are, to various extents, likewise
necessary, but less frequently incurred and (with the exception of clothing) usually
much higher in value than day-to-day expenses such as those on food, housing,
communication, and transportation. It thus makes sense for households to use windfall
incomes on such big-ticket items.
Finally, expenditures on nondurables, leisure, and special occasions do not seem to
increase by much with additional permanent and transitory income, suggesting that
these items do not fall within the realm of priorities of lower- and middle-class
households. These consumption categories are arguably inessential (leisure and
special occasions more so than nondurables) and are also less frequently incurred. In
addition, leisure products and services may also be quite expensive, making them
luxuries that lower- and middle-class households cannot readily afford.
The results suggest that lower- and middle-class households do try to allocate
additional incomes responsibly. I see that large portions of additional income, either
permanent or transitory (but more so for the former than the latter) tend to go toward
items that most households cannot live decently without. Unfortunately, apparent
attempts at prudence notwithstanding, low permanent income bases coupled with
high costs of necessities make it difficult for these households to incur savings
sustainably.
Before I conclude this discussion, I must tackle a few issues regarding my empirical
strategy. While the results of my empirical tests are useful and generally consistent with
theoretical expectations and economic realities, I accept that there are theoretical and
empirical limitations that constrain me from performing such analyses that are fully
captures all economic phenomena surrounding the matter.

permanent income for wealthier households, hence the trends in income composition
shown in Figure 2. Intergenerational wealth persistence among upper class
households should lead me to conclude that larger portions of their observed income

6 Implications for policy and research
If income—permanent income, specifically—is so crucial to saving, policy interventions
will have to revolve around raising permanent income levels or managing expenditure
pressures of households to effectively increase permanent disposable income levels. It
is also imperative for national government authorities to assess the policies explicitly
geared towards promoting saving among households and those that only tangentially
affect household saving by how they affect permanent income.
However, policymakers’ immediate influence on household incomes is limited; unless
macroeconomic circumstances make it feasible to provide universal basic income or to
occasional and deliberately introduce transitory shocks to income to households, there
is little they can do to increase the permanent income of households at an immediately
tangible level. Developmental interventions to increase the permanent income of
households, such as investing heavily in human capital development or creating more
employment opportunities, may indeed allow households to break the permanency of
poverty, but the returns on these investments take time to materialize.

Thus, Philippine policymakers must explore how to alleviate expenditure-side
pressures of households to effectively raise the (permanent) disposable incomes of
households at the aggregate level.
On the fiscal side, this means ensuring that product supply chains, especially for basic
goods and services, are intact and undisrupted to prevent cost-push inflationary
shocks. Fiscal authorities could also promote saving at a large scale by improving basic
economic infrastructure and systems such as public education, socialized healthcare,
and mass transportation, among others, to ease the financial burdens of households.
They should also consider income inequality in raising consumption taxes, particularly
those on basic commodities that are universally necessary (regardless of class);
increasing the tax burdens on consumers will obviously be more damaging for poorer
households whose incomes are already permanently low.
On the monetary side, promoting household saving entails maintaining low and stable
inflation, particularly of basic commodities, by prudently managing aggregate demand
through sound monetary policy. Though this is something the country’s central
monetary authority has already been doing by default, being that it is among the BSP’s
primary mandates, it bears noting as the impetus for carefully-calibrated monetary
policy is emphasized.
The BSP also has more specific interventions to induce further household saving that
are in line with its financial inclusion agenda. There are consumer-side interventions
that aim to address shortcomings in demand for formal financial products and services.
There are also supply-side interventions that rectify the lack of suitable financial
products and services for those who remain to be unserved and underserved.
On the demand side, the BSP regularly conducts financial education and literacy
campaigns to induce greater formal financial participation among Philippine citizens.10
In these campaigns, the BSP encourages participants to observe proper financial
planning and budgeting; one such way is by promoting the “correct” saving formula,
wherein a portion of income is allocated for savings first to determine expenditures
(instead of savings being the residual of income and expenditures), in these
campaigns. The BSP also educates them about available financial services offered by
formal financial institutions.
The BSP in 2018 also issued circular introducing a framework that allows banks to offer
basic deposit accounts to unserved and underserved consumers.11 A basic deposit
account features a “liberalized customer onboarding”, low initial deposit and zero
maintaining balance requirements, and no dormancy charges. In other words, a basic
deposit account is designed to be free of transactional frictions at and beyond the
account opening stage.

the effectiveness of such interventions on the most vulnerable households given the
overarching importance of permanent income to household saving. For example,

My results indicate that there are areas of opportunity for policymakers to promote
household saving at the behavioral level, however. First, I find preliminary evidence of
prudence among lower- and middle-class households with respect to how they spend
their incomes. Second, I see that the aforesaid households are likely to increase their
savings when they experience transitory income shocks, suggesting that their saving
habits are driven by precautionary motives. It seems, then, that campaigns to enhance
household budgeting and to encourage households to smooth their consumption may
enable households to develop fruitful mindsets with regard to saving.
Along with existing ones in the literature, this study builds the case for the collection of
more frequent and more exhaustive data to monitor the household saving situation in
the Philippines. One such opportunity for enhanced data collection comes in the form
of the Consumer Finance Survey (CFS) series of the BSP. The CFS series consists of
more wealth-focused surveys that record more extensive accounts of household assets
and liabilities in addition to current income and expenditure flows. These surveys have
the potential to capture arguably better ideas of the permanent incomes of
households. It would be considerably useful to design future iterations of the CFS with
such theoretical frameworks as the permanent income hypothesis in mind. Additional
variables that may serve as better instruments for permanent income will surely be
welcome additions for future researchers to use and appreciate.
7 Conclusion
In this study, I assess Philippine household saving behaviors through the lens of the
permanent income hypothesis (Friedman, 1957) and under the context of income
inequality. The low-savings and high-inequality macroeconomic situation in the
Philippines motivates my inquiry.
I formulate an empirical model based on the instrumental variable method to estimate
permanent and transitory income and determine how these two income components
affect the household saving rate across income classes. As do previous studies in the
literature, I find that using observed income measures to test the income-saving
relationship tends to overstate how income affects household saving. I also find that,
based on my empirical tests, the observed incomes of lower- and middle-class
households are mostly permanent, but those of lower-class households are, in fact, low
relative to expected levels based on my estimates. Notably, I find that lower- and
middle-class households tend to consume large portions of additional permanent

income for essential and regular expenses (suggesting prudence in spending) enough
to actually decrease their saving rates.
My results indicate that inequalities in permanent income should be a central
consideration when it comes to designing policies to encourage greater saving among
Philippine households. There is also cause to reevaluate how effective existing policies
are based on how they address or incorporate the implications of the permanent
income hypothesis on household saving.
This study offers a preliminary analysis on the role of permanent income inequality on
Philippine household savings, but much remains to be learned. In particular, there is a
behavioral aspect to household spending and saving that this study cannot explore
given certain data constraints. Because of the seemingly diminished predictive power
of the model for upper-class households, there is also room for further analysis with
regard to the saving behaviors of the rich. Thus, future research stands to benefit from
enhancements in data collection. These limitations notwithstanding, this study provides
value to government authorities and contributes to extant literature as it underscores
the need to consider the poor in crafting policies and in conducting empirical inquiries.

Annex B  Supplemental tests using alternate instruments for permanent
income
I test the robustness of my main results by conducting additional tests using alternate
instruments for permanent income. As discussed in Section 4, household consumption
(Zabot & Gomes, 2021; Alan et al., 2015; Dynan et al., 2004) and real asset ownership
(Bebczuk et al., 2015) are provided as additional instruments.
I consider two forms of consumption as potential instruments. I first follow the line of
reasoning of the permanent income hypothesis and take total consumption as my
instrument. If consumption were largely driven by permanent income, then observed
consumption should suffice as an instrument so long as I understand that transitory
consumption shocks and measurement errors in consumption will bias the marginal
effect of permanent income downward (Zabot & Gomes, 2021; Dynan et al., 2004).
Next, I consider more specific categories of consumption that are likely to be driven
more by permanent income, rather than transitory. Zabot & Gomes (2021) and Alan et
al. (2015) use all forms of nondurable consumption, but I take a more specific
approach and single out the categories of consumption I identify to be essential and
frequently incurred: food, housing, communication, and transportation. I use the sum
of these four consumption categories as my alternate consumption instrument.

Contrary to my primary findings, the coefficient estimates for permanent income
remain negative across all subsamples, suggesting that the marginal propensities to
consume out of permanent income are large enough for all households across the
income distribution to cause dissaving. Though I question the validity of this
proposition, I nonetheless find value in confirming that using observed income values
to test the relationship between income and the household saving rate overstates the
estimated effect of the former on the latter.
I also consider two additional instruments related to home ownership. First, I use home
ownership alone as the instrument. (I explain why in Section 4.) I also follow the
reasoning behind using consumption as an instrument but narrow down my focus to
regular expenses related to home ownership that may also be positively correlated
with permanent income: real property tax. I intuit that using a specific portion of
consumption only induces a smaller bias than using the total amount. I also attempt to
strengthen the predictive power of the model for upper-class households based on the
intuition that higher-income households pay more in property tax. I thus use this
variable as an instrument in addition to my original instrument and home ownership.
Using both home ownership variables as instruments for permanent income seem to
have almost the same effect as using the college educational attainment of the
household head alone for the same purpose. Figure 7 shows that the resulting
permanent income estimates using home ownership instruments are also very close to
my original estimates, but adding the real property tax instrument yields slightly higher
permanent income estimates for upper-class households.
