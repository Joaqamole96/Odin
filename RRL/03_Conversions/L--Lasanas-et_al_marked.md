Filipino family dynamics and culture may influence how traditional values like
“utang na loob” (debt of gratitude) are understood across time. The purpose
of the study is to understand the concept of utang na loob in the Southern
Philippines from the perspective of working panganays (eldest children). The
research investigated the narratives of the eldest child in the Filipino family
regarding their experiences with utang na loob. It employed Murray’s three
levels of narrative analysis as a framework and elicited themes on personal,
positional, and ideological levels. Results revealed personal experiences of
early maturity and filial pressure, gratitude, and fulfillment, among others. The
positional narratives complement the personal narratives as they describe
their roles as second parents, financial providers, and decision-makers.
Similarly, their ideological narratives revealed internalized familial obligations
shaped by cultural expectations, empowered responsibility arising from
adversity to provide family support, and increased hope in the capacity to
cultivate a better future for one’s family. Implications of the findings regarding
community interventions to address difficulties in socioeconomic disparities,
family responsibilities, and personal development are discussed.

1. INTRODUCTION
Families often establish bonds that value close family ties and
solidarity rooted in the Asian characteristics of Filipinos, where
harmony and interdependence are valued, creating a deeply felt
relationship in families (Dy 2003). It comes as no surprise that
Filipinos are ready to go far and beyond their own boundaries
for the sake of closeness and devotion to their families. This
results in extensive pressure on the eldest children to display
qualities of being responsible, obedient, and stable. Most of the
eldest children are perceived as the backbone of the sibling order,
resulting in eldest children having a hard time saying no to their
families when asked for favors (Case 2011). In the Philippines, it
is a common practice for the eldest child to be the second parent
to their siblings (Jumalon et al. 2023). In times of difficulties,
the eldest children have often carried the burden of looking
for means for the family’s survival through financial assistance,
emotional support, or physical presence.
The implications of expectations for one’s children and the
weight of being the eldest lie in the value of “utang na loob” or
debt of gratitude owed to another person through expressions
of deep respect and gratitude (Enriquez 1994). Reyes (2015)
argues that utang na loob is the self-imposed obligation to give
back the same kind of beauty-of-will a person has shown you.
In a family setting, parents are responsible for childbearing
and child-rearing, often sacrificing much of their time, effort,
and resources. However, it is a common phenomenon among
Filipinos that children are expected to possess utang na loob
toward their parents through honoring the family obligations
and giving back to their families.
Previous studies explored the experiences of eldest children in
the context of Filipino values. However, they have not addressed
how these experiences affect the eldest children throughout their
lives. In this paper, we would like to address research gaps and gain
extensive insights regarding the narratives of working panganays
(eldest children) and their experiences of utang na loob in their
families, which previous research has not delved into. Through an

analysis of these experiences, this study aims to re-evaluate the
long-standing accounts of utang na loob in families, focusing on
the present times.
FILIPINO FAMILY DYNAMICS
The Philippines, as an interdependent country, follows
collectivist characteristics that emphasize others and social
support, especially in decision-making (Broomhall, Phillips,
and Wu 2020). Specifically, the Filipino family setting promotes
high values of reciprocity, which observes extended kinship (Go
1993; Medina 2001; and Miralao 1997 cited in Tarroja 2010). In
this context, the family is considered the primary unit of society.
Many social behaviors are thereby geared toward maintaining
harmony and unity within this structure.
The concept of utang na loob is fundamental to
understanding Filipino family dynamics. This cultural value
dictates that individuals are morally obligated to repay those
who have provided them with significant help, particularly
family members. This cultural expectation places a significant
responsibility on children, especially the eldest, to contribute
to the family’s welfare, both emotionally and economically. This
collective orientation shapes behaviors such as providing support
for family members in times of need, including extending
financial help and making personal sacrifices for the sake of
family harmony (Dizon et al. 2024). These values are passed on
intergenerationally, maintaining the stability and cohesion of
Filipino families across different contexts.
In the present, the practice of utang na loob continues to
hold its original intent for some families, but how it is introduced
and the expectations placed on firstborns have significantly
influenced its application within Filipino households. According
to Baculi, Garapo, and Polvoriza (2022), the way this value is
passed down shapes how it is practiced, particularly by the eldest
children who often bear the weight of familial obligations tied
to utang na loob.

UTANG NA LOOB IN SIKOLOHIYANG PILIPINO
“Sikolohiyang Pilipino” or “Filipino psychology” describes
psychology that is derived from Filipino experience, thought,
and orientation and is founded on the full usage of the Filipino
language and culture (Rungduin et al. 2016). One of the most
important Filipino values, utang na loob is essential to preserving
interpersonal ties, especially in family systems. The concept
originates from Sikolohiyang Pilipino, which emphasizes the
moral need to return kindness. According to Rungduin et al.
(2016), utang na loob is frequently defined by pagtutumbas
(reciprocity) and pagkilala (acknowledgment) in which the
beneficiary of a favor feels profoundly beholden even if the giver
does not specifically request reimbursement. As recipients work
to return acts of kindness, whether materially or by continuing
to show kindness to others, this reciprocal relationship fortifies
social ties. The fundamental idea is a wider feeling of panlipunang
gampanin (social duty) that encompasses the community as well
as personal thankfulness.
In Sikolohiyang Pilipino, utang na loob is understood as
a multifaceted value that intertwines with emotional, social,
and cultural dynamics. Unlike Western notions of gratitude,
which are often associated with positive emotions, utang na
loob can evoke complex feelings because it may create implicit
social pressure to fulfill obligations even when it is difficult. As
noted by Dancel (2005, 109–127), this value transcends mere
thankfulness, becoming an integral part of the Filipino psyche
that motivates prosocial behaviors, such as helping one’s family
and community.
THE ELDEST CHILD AS WORKING PANGANAY
The role of the eldest child in Filipino families, often referred
to as the panganay, carries significant responsibilities, especially
in families with limited financial resources. This social dynamic
is deeply embedded in the Filipino value system, where familial
loyalty and interdependence are prioritized. Research has shown

that the panganay is frequently expected to take on a caregiving
role for younger siblings, acting as a surrogate parent in many
ways. Medina (2001) emphasizes that the panganay often
assumes both caregiving and disciplinary duties, a responsibility
driven by cultural expectations and the hierarchical nature of
Filipino families. These expectations are more pronounced in
lower-income families, where the financial contributions of the
eldest child become crucial for the family’s survival.
In addition to caregiving, the eldest child is also expected
to contribute financially, sometimes at the expense of their
ambitions. A study by Alampay and Jocson (2011) reveals that
many Filipino panganays take on early employment to support
their siblings’ education, a practice driven by the collective
orientation of Filipino families. Eldest children may sacrifice their
own educational opportunities or career aspirations to prioritize
the needs of their siblings and parents. The social pressure to
conform to these familial roles is heightened by utang na loob.
We have identified some gaps across different studies that tackle
the same topic of interest, such as contrasting intergenerational
perspectives. There is limited literature exploring how the
experience of utang na loob varies across generations. The
expectations placed on panganays in traditional Filipino families
may differ significantly between older generations and those in
contemporary settings. For instance, while Nagtalon-Ramos
(2020) examined intergenerational experiences of utang na loob
among Filipino American nurses, few studies have focused on
this aspect within the Filipino family structure. Investigating
these intergenerational differences could address a crucial gap in
understanding how family expectations evolve over time.
Further, we acknowledge the rise of modern technology
and the internet, which may also present a research gap. Brey
(2006) highlights concerns that the internet poses risks to
social structures, culture, and human relationships. As such, the
widespread use of the internet and social media among younger
generations has introduced new forms of communication
that may affect how traditional values like utang na loob are
understood. While older generations rely on face-to-face

2. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES AND FRAMEWORK
As observed in the existing literature, there has been a temporal
gap wherein a lack of recent research has left a discontinuity
in understanding this particular phenomenon, considering
variations across intergenerational perspectives. This study thus
sought to address this gap in the literature by acknowledging
how utang na loob keeps pace with the latest perspectives of
working panganays in the present generation through the use of
narrative analysis.
The structure, content, and purpose of the stories people
tell during socialization are the center of narrative psychology
(Murray 2003). It acknowledges that we live in a world of stories
and that we translate the stories we tell each other to understand
the behavior of both ourselves and other people. We utilized
Murray’s (2000) framework as a guide to elicit three levels of
narrative analysis: personal (experiences), positional (roles and
responsibilities), and ideological (shared values and beliefs).
Murray further argued that within the discipline of health
psychology, narratives serve as crucial tools for making sense of
illness, recovery, and coping. Through storytelling, individuals
do not simply recount events but actively construct meaning,
integrate disruptions caused by illness or trauma into their life
stories, and preserve a sense of identity and coherence. This
interpretive process highlights how health and well-being are
shaped not only by biological or psychological factors but also by
the meanings individuals assign to their lived experiences.
The personal level of analysis examined how working
panganays perceive their personal experiences of utang na loob.
The positional level of analysis examined how the narrative
presents the working panganay’s responsibilities and the roles
assigned by family members. The social representations of
78 Social Transformations Journal of the Global South Vol. 12, No. 1, May 2024

learned values in the narratives were examined at the ideological
level of analysis.
Since working panganays have more responsibilities
compared to their younger siblings in Filipino families, it is
important to understand their perspective because these could
influence how they carry themselves and manage multiple roles
in the household. Specifically, we asked:
1. What personal experiences do working panganays
have with utang na loob?
2. What positions are assigned to working panganays
by their parents, siblings, and themselves?
3. What ideologies are reflected in the working
panganays’ narratives of their overall experience?
3. METHODS AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
This study examined the narratives of working panganays
concerning the concept of utang na loob. Driven by the purpose
of bringing together an “organized interpretation of a sequence
of events” (Murray 2003, 113 cited in Willig 2008, 133), the
study employed narrative analysis as a framework for the data
analysis. We utilized a qualitative research design to construct
broad interview questions to process thoroughly guided data
collection. The study gathered data from eight individuals
through purposive sampling. Participants are the eldest children
in the family who have personal experience working to provide
financial support for their family members. All participants
reside in the Southern Philippines, with four female and four
male respondents between the ages of 25 to 40 years old with
current occupations as virtual assistant, barangay kagawad
(councilor), marine engineer, guidance counselor, human resource
professional, and health education and promotion officer, earning
in the low income to upper middle income. The respondents hail
from Surigao City, Zamboanga del Sur, Lanao del Sur, Misamis
Oriental, and Cagayan de Oro City.
Semi-structured interviews were utilized with broad
questions to allow the interviewees to construct their narratives.

These questions were validated by a senior member of the research
team. Probing questions were used to further clarify responses.
The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim with all
identifying information removed. A total of seven (7) questions
were constructed for the interview. These questions were aimed
at asking the participant about their journey as a working
panganay supporting the family [item 1]; their experiences
as the panganay in the family and their responsibilities [item
2]; the expectations placed by parents and siblings [item 3];
their definition of utang na loob [item 4]; and the reflections,
challenges, and insights gathered by the panganay based on their
unique experiences [item 5, 6, and 7]. English, Cebuano, and
Surigaonon translations were included in the interview guide to
accommodate the participants’ preferred language.
Online advertisements in the form of a “call for participants”
post shared through social media platforms (i.e., Facebook)
were used to gather the participants for this study. Before the
virtual interview, participants were contacted and asked to
sign the informed consent form that disclosed the nature and
purpose of the study as well as the conditions of participation.
“Pakikipagkuwentuhan” or storytelling was employed as an
Indigenous research method to ground Filipino psychology
within this study. This approach enabled researchers to engage
with participants in a conversational manner that encouraged
them to share personal narratives relevant to the study’s focus,
maintaining a culturally resonant and open dialogue (Orteza
1997). To ensure the online safety of both researchers and the
participants, interviews were conducted via secure, encrypted
video conferencing platforms. The audio recording of the
interview sessions was transcribed and used only for data
analysis. Furthermore, strict compliance with the Psychological
Association of the Philippines (PAP) stipulation on research
ethics was followed.
Thematic analysis following the analytical framework of
Braun and Clarke (2014) was conducted by the researchers.
Common patterns were extracted from the raw data and initial
codes were developed. Following this, codes were organized

4A. BEING THE PANGANAY
EXPERIENCE OF EARLY MATURITY
AND FILIAL PRESSURE
In their early years, panganays have acknowledged their
socioeconomic status to make certain adjustments in the guiding
factors of their actions, particularly in the attainment of their
respective goals. That is, their identity is shaped over time based
on what is expected of their roles as the working panganay. Thus,
they consider such experiences as their preparation for the later
stage of being the eldest child as presented below:
“I was not a self-proclaimed breadwinner, but I could
somehow say that I was being prepared slowly.”
(Participant 5)
“I am already aware of my status in life that I was not
born into a well-off family.” (Participant 3)
Furthermore, panganays bear the filial pressure, which their
parents and caregivers expect of them. This expectation leads to
early maturity in terms of how they perceive the realities of life
and their responsibility to the family. Resultingly, they were able
to commit to an extension of responsibility, especially to their
younger siblings.
EXPERIENCE OF GRATITUDE FOR A COLLECTIVE
EFFORT TOWARD AN EDUCATION
In recollections of their early life, panganays viewed their
experience of attaining an education from a rooted appreciation
for the communal support provided by their immediate and
extended family members. This gratitude goes beyond simple
acknowledgment because they recognize their educational
journey has been a collective effort. It involved sacrifices from
parents and relatives who provided financial support.

“When I reached 4th year, from high school to
college, it was my uncle, who is like my father,
who sent me to college . . . So, they were the ones
who paid for my education, and I am very thankful
for them because they really supported me in my
studies back then in college.” (Participant 2)
Through such gratitude, panganays acknowledged a profound
duty to reciprocate the assistance they received by dedicating
themselves to their studies and eventually stepping into the role
of a provider for their families.
“Actually, those people who helped me—up until
now—I make sure to gradually show my appreciation
to them, so that I can give something back to them,
even just a little because they helped me through
really difficult times.” (Participant 1)
The narratives above illustrate how panganays frame
family achievements as shared successes. Yet, this gratitude
often transforms into an internalized obligation to give back,
sometimes at the cost of their own personal aspirations. The
analysis highlights how these conflicting thoughts, emotions,
and actions coexist as gratitude for collective support in
attaining an education and are inseparable from the sense of
indebtedness to those who sacrificed for them. This coexistence
reflects a narrative tension in which appreciation and burden are
not opposing forces but intertwined dynamics that shape how
panganays construct their identity and sense of purpose.
POSITIONING OF PARENTS AS UNABLE TO FULLY
PROVIDE FOR THE NEEDS OF THE FAMILY
Even at a very young age, panganays have viewed their parents as
unable to fully provide for the financial and emotional needs of
the family. This prompted their belief to look for ways to sustain
their own needs and their family’s needs at an early age. This
is manifested through the panganays’ attempts to either look
for scholarships to continue their education without inflicting

POSITIONING AS PANGANAY WHO ACTS
AS THE SECOND PARENT TO YOUNGER SIBLINGS
IN CHILD-REARING
Panganays are always deemed as the responsible ones in the
family even at an early age. They are expected to have learned to
adopt a stance of preparedness for uncertainties, framing their
hardships as grounds for taking up future responsibilities. This
role that they play extends to their siblings. The parents of such
panganays would turn over, if not, share the load of child-rearing
responsibilities to the panganay as they amass more financial
capabilities and resources. They would stand as the provider
for their sibling’s education and even their sibling’s desires and
wants. This is shown in the attempt of Participant 4 to spare
some extra allowance for her sister:
“As a student, when I had extra [money], I would
even give that to my sister because sometimes she
has several requirements in her course. I would feel
pity every time she will ask from our parents. So, if I
had extra money or extra allowance, I would give it
to her.”
This common practice has shown stark differences among
various socio-economic groups. For those in seemingly

middle-class households, panganays are willing to bear this
responsibility. However, this shows a conflicting reaction in
panganays in lower-income households whose families solely
rely on them. They emit a more aggrieved reaction toward
this responsibility because their help extends not only to their
siblings but also to their own separate families. This is apparent
in the statement of Participant 1:
“I have so many responsibilities to bear: my sibling,
my younger sibling, and the newborn child of my
younger sibling who is around 2 years old. So that’s
why I have so many [financial] responsibilities to
provide for diapers, milk, food, and wipes. So there’s
a lot.”
INTERNALIZED FAMILIAL OBLIGATIONS SHAPED
BY CULTURAL EXPECTATIONS IDEOLOGY
Filipino values and cultural expectations are inseparable from the
panganay’s view of themselves. Early in life, they have gained an
awareness of the value of prioritizing their families’ needs above
their own. This internalized ideology has shaped them to form
an independent sense of self that is built on fulfilling the familial
duties expected of them. Such duties have been reinforced by
the panganay’s own interactions with their parents or guardians.
While other obligations may not have been stated explicitly, they
feel that their parents have been rather implicit in other duties as
mentioned by the participants.
“My mother always tells me and has instilled in me
that a person who endures will become blessed.”
(Participant 1)
“I really feel that I have to give them something
because they might feel bad.” (Participant 3)
Panganays also expressed their early experiences in embedding
the cultural expectation of the need to build oneself to give back
to their families. Such filial pressures as perceived by panganays

doing so, their sense of self is defined less by having autonomy
in making decisions for themselves, but rather it is more about
sharing the collective responsibilities within the family.
4B. DEALING WITH MAJOR LIFE EVENTS
EXPERIENCE OF STEPPING UP
FROM A FAMILY CRISIS
Transitioning to the middle phase in life, panganays experienced
a turning point when they stepped up from a family crisis. This
has led to the immediate acceptance of additional tasks in the
household, leaving them with no choice but to take on the
responsibility as the panganay. Specifically, this was evident in
86 Social Transformations Journal of the Global South Vol. 12, No. 1, May 2024

the event of a medical condition or death of a family member as
mentioned below:
“The hospital could not handle it because my
father’s tumor got bigger. So somehow, we were still
confused as a family with what was happening. So
the big finances, big projects or needs of the family,
I was the one to provide for my father’s maintenance
and everything . . . it changes you.” (Participant 5)
“When I lost my father—he died—I was in first
year college at that time. When I realized that at
that moment, I pondered on the weight of my
responsibility as an eldest child because since
then I was able to grasp my overall responsibility.”
(Participant 6)
In many families, this experience compels the panganay to
step into a more mature role, sometimes adopting responsibilities
far beyond their years. They may need to support younger siblings
emotionally, manage household tasks, or even help with financial
concerns, placing them in a caretaker role that accelerates their
maturity. This shift can instill resilience and a heightened sense
of empathy as they become more attuned to the emotional needs
of others. However, the weight of these new responsibilities can
also bring emotional challenges, such as feeling overwhelmed
due to the expectation of being “strong” for the family.
POSITIONING OF YOUNGER SIBLINGS
WHO ARE DEPENDENT ON THE ELDEST
CHILD FOR GUIDANCE
It is emphasized that panganays are thought to have a duty to
nurture and mentor their younger siblings, especially in their
middle chapter. They frequently talked about having a strong
sense of duty to act as role models for their younger siblings,
providing them with both practical and emotional support.
This dependence strengthens their sense of responsibility and
establishes their position as the family’s pillar of support.

For Participant 2, the pressure of being the panganay and

“I was the one who graduated from college. I felt the

EMPOWERED RESPONSIBILITY
ARISING FROM ADVERSITY TO PROVIDE
FAMILY SUPPORT IDEOLOGY
This theme emphasizes the sense of empowerment that working
panganays experience as a result of their early adversity that
was brought into the middle stage of life. They view their duty
as a source of strength and moral obligation. They take pride
in meeting family standards despite making major personal
sacrifices. Their identification as the family’s cornerstone is
strengthened by redefining their struggle of financial hardship,
unfulfilled personal aspirations, or a lack of familial support as
sources of resilience. Participant 2 pointed out that because of
the need to care for the family, their primary emphasis is now
meeting their role as the primary provider rather than pursuing
personal financial gain:
“It’s pressure on me because when it comes to
finances, I really feel like it’s time for me not only to
purely think of my own financial situation.”

Despite the weight of the obligation, it is also incredibly
empowering. Being able to provide for their families, even at
the expense of personal sacrifice, is often a source of fulfillment
and meaning for working panganays. For them, being able to
provide, no matter the personal cost, is enough to make them
happy. Family crises often compel panganays to step into adult
roles early on, taking on the challenge of handling financial and
emotional responsibilities that position them as both providers
and role models. This burden can be heavy, but it also fosters
resilience and empowerment as fulfilling family needs become a
central source of meaning in their lives.

The narratives of the study share a commonality in their
conceptualization of utang na loob as manifested in a meaningful
sense of “returning the sacrifices and favors out of the goodness
of their heart” that have been granted to them by their families.
In this study, we explored the personal experiences, positions, and
ideologies of the working panganays to gauge their collective
perceptions of the said Filipino psychological construct. Themes
across the narratives included immense gratitude, feelings of love,
and a strengthened will to reciprocate the hardships their families
have gone through to help them achieve their goals (i.e., completing
their education). Panganays feel a deep sense of fulfillment and
satisfaction in reaching a stage where they are capable of providing
more for their families. However, while one cannot fully eradicate
the existing social pressures to conform to one’s moral obligation in
taking the familial role as a primary provider (Alampay and Jocson
2011; Rungduin et al. 2016), the panganays of this study rooted
their perceptions of utang na loob in one’s personal desire and
commitment to give back. As such, the discoveries collated from
the narratives promoted one’s willingness and one’s intrinsic desire
for the ability as a ‘provider’, which allows them to gain fulfillment
from supporting the younger siblings and other members of their
families (Zajonc 1976).
The results indicate that working panganays engage in self-
reflection on their past personal experiences that have shaped
their current being. The recognition of such experiences has
granted them both gratitude and the ability to reflect on the
steps they need to take to repay their respective families. A
number of the participants, when asked about their concept
of utang na loob, mention this as a selfless form of providing
for their families. Although not removing the concept from its
original meaning in Filipino psychology (Enriquez 1994; Kaut
1961), many panganays view their support as an act of altruism
and self-sacrifice. This perspective reflects a deep commitment to
one’s familial well-being, where personal desires take secondary
priority to achieving the family’s collective needs. A key point
for these working panganays is the belief in a sense of duty to
provide for their families. Similar to the ideology of Rungduin

et al. (2016) on utang na loob, panganays view their ability to
give back as stemming from a social duty to reciprocate for the
beneficiaries despite not specifically requesting reimbursement.
Thus, working panganays have embraced this responsibility
without any regret, viewing their support as a heartfelt way to
express love and profound gratitude for the sacrifices made to
help them reach their goals.
Furthermore, there are important insights into the implications
of utang na loob in Filipino family dynamics that demonstrate its
significant influence on the duties and responsibilities of working
panganays in the Southern Philippines. Panganays are positioned
as both providers and caregivers due to their internalization of
familial responsibilities, which are influenced by cultural norms.
Reiterating studies on how utang na loob can impede personal
goals when family expectations become excessively demanding,
this can lead to substantial psychological and financial stress,
even while it generates strong intergenerational support (Dizon
et al. 2024; Rungduin et al. 2016).
Utang na loob has changed in contemporary settings to
represent the conflict between the past collectivist beliefs and the
emergence of individualistic desires among newer generations.
Panganays are moving toward “enlightened loyalty,” where
reciprocation is based on voluntary caring rather than guilt-
driven obligation, as a result of urbanization and social media
exposure (Gohu 2022). This change promotes striking a balance
between upholding family obligations and achieving individual
objectives. However, putting family needs first frequently
prevents panganays from moving up the social ladder because
opportunities and money are diverted to family responsibilities,
which maintains socioeconomic disparities (Lagon 2024).
Based on the narratives, families may change how utang na
loob is used by redefining it as an act of love and respect rather
than a heavy debt. Panganays might feel less psychologically
burdened by such a viewpoint, enabling them to accept their
duties with satisfaction rather than bitterness (Dancel 2005).
Filipino families, then, can modify utang na loob to contemporary
situations while maintaining its cultural integrity by encouraging
voluntary cooperation and respect for one another.

Interventions in the community and policy are necessary
to address the issues that eldest children face. Programs that
emphasize career development, financial literacy, and mental
health assistance can give eldest children the tools they need
to carry out their responsibilities well. Campaigns for public
awareness might also inform families about the advantages
of sharing responsibilities fairly, which would lessen the
psychological and socioeconomic strains associated with utang
na loob. Filipino families may ensure that everyone’s well-being
is attained without compromising personal goals by placing this
cultural value inside modern frameworks.
5. LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
This study highlights the deeply ingrained cultural concept
of utang na loob and how it significantly affects the lives of
working panganays in the Southern Philippines. Results show
how panganays internalize their roles as caregivers and providers,
motivated by a deep sense of obligation to return the sacrifices
made by their families as well as by a great deal of love and gratitude.
Many panganays accept their obligations as acts of selflessness
and personal fulfillment despite the difficulties presented by
societal and familial expectations. This reflects a balance between
traditional values and growing individualistic viewpoints.
The narratives reflect how utang na loob is changing in
contemporary Filipino society, where ideas of “enlightened
loyalty” promote sincere and choice reciprocation rather than
duty. Nonetheless, the strain of family obligations frequently
restricts chances for individual development and intensifies
socioeconomic disparities. Families can lessen psychological
strain and create a more positive dynamic that promotes both
individual and group well-being by redefining utang na loob as a
shared act of love and respect.
It is essential to encourage family cooperation and shared
responsibility to adapt utang na loob to modern situations. This
will create a culture in which help is provided freely and fairly.
Furthermore, panganays can better manage their dual roles

if policy and community actions address the emotional and
financial difficulties they encounter. Future studies that include
a wider range of socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds will
improve our knowledge of this deeply rooted Filipino value and
ensure it keeps fostering family ties without impeding personal
development. Considerations for incorporating mixed method
approaches along with ethnographic methodological tools can
provide a more comprehensive understanding of any emerging
patterns. This study also extends recommendations to consider
broader contexts, mixed methods, and practical interventions for

A limitation of the study was the saturation of the participants

views of urban-born individuals and those who were raised in
rural areas. This might answer the limitation on contradicting
experiences and viewpoints of utang na loob in panganays since
varying family dynamics are deeply rooted in one’s culture.
